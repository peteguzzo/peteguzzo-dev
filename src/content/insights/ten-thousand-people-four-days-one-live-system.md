Back in April, Quest Diagnostics came to us trying to figure out how to upgrade a national town hall tied to their Southeast conference.

They had looked at conference rooms, hotels and a few other options, but they wanted it to feel more like an actual broadcast -- not ten executives sitting around a table on a webcam.

The audience was roughly 10,000 employees watching live.

At that point, we weren't new, but Studio G had only fully opened in February. We were still getting the opportunity to show what the entire facility could really do when you connected the stages, the control room, the creator spaces and the technology behind all of it.

And while this wasn't some giant network broadcast like some of the work we're doing now, technically it wasn't any less challenging.

Maybe more so.

Because almost everything had to work inside Microsoft Teams and Quest's own secured laptops and network environment.

The main town hall had about ten Quest executives and board members in Studio A with a moderator.

At the same time, another Quest team member was set up separately in our Creator Suite talking with executives and guests joining remotely from other parts of the country and the world -- including a Paralympic champion, which was pretty damn cool.

So think about what was actually happening.

We were sending the live program out through Teams to thousands of employees while simultaneously bringing remote Teams feeds back into our control room.

Teams isn't designed to look like television.

It wants to show interface elements, speaker windows, thumbnails and all the stuff that makes sense when you're sitting in a normal Teams meeting -- but not when you're trying to make something look like a broadcast.

Using the effects capabilities in our Blackmagic ATEM system, we were able to reframe those incoming feeds, push past a lot of the Teams look and create clean split screens between the executives sitting in Studio A and the people joining remotely.

At the same time, Quest was running an interactive audience platform so employees could vote, respond and participate live.

Employees could also call in and ask questions directly to the executives.

So now we had multiple things happening at once:

Our local multi-camera production.

Incoming remote video.

Outgoing live transmission.

Split-screen effects.

Audience interaction.

Live callers.

Graphics.

Audio routing.

Monitoring.

All happening in real time.

Audio was probably one of the trickier parts.

When the CEO or another executive was speaking with somebody remotely, everyone else sitting there still needed to hear that person too.

We couldn't just put one executive on an IFB and call it a day because the rest of the board needed to participate in the conversation.

So we had to create a room-monitoring workflow where everybody could hear the remote guest without creating a feedback loop back through Teams.

We were also filtering and managing some of that incoming audio live.

And because Quest is a major healthcare company with serious security requirements, we couldn't just plug whatever we wanted into their system.

We were working through several Quest-issued laptops and their approved environment while still tying everything back into our control-room workflow.

This went on for four days.

And here's the funny part.

We had one real test the day before.

And that test didn't come anywhere close to representing everything that was actually about to happen.

Not because anybody screwed anything up.

Quest had never done something exactly like this before either.

Andrea and her team were figuring out parts of the format while we were figuring out the technical workflow with them.

So we walked through it together, adjusted things in real time and got it done.

By the time everything was running, Quest executives and employees were watching the broadcast everywhere in the building -- on the kitchen TVs, green-room monitors and throughout the studio -- while thousands more were watching remotely.

That's the part I remember.

If you weren't part of Quest, the town hall itself probably wouldn't mean much to you.

But if you understand production, what was happening behind that screen was a hell of a thing.

We weren't just streaming a meeting.

We were taking a corporate platform that wasn't designed to look like television and turning it into a four-day interactive live broadcast -- with local talent, remote guests, audience participation, incoming and outgoing feeds, live audio management and a control room holding the entire thing together.

And we got it done.

That was the win.
