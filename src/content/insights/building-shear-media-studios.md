Most studios get designed for the walkthrough.

The lighting is good. The cyclorama is clean. The lounge photographs well for the listing. Then three productions try to use the building on the same day and the whole thing turns into a logistics problem because nobody designed it for that.

I've built two studios now, 211 Studio and Shear Media Studios, and they both taught me the same lesson before I was done: a studio isn't a room. It's an operating system. The room is just the part you can see.

## What Shear Media Studios actually is

Shear Media Studios is an 11,000 sq. ft. multi-studio facility in St. Petersburg, FL. Soundstages, podcast and influencer suites, live-shopping environments, a control room, multi-stream event production capability.

I designed it and stood it up. Not as a contractor, not as a consultant on someone else's build. I built it as the person responsible for making it generate revenue from day one.

That changes every single decision.

## The question that drives the whole design

Before you touch a floor plan, you need to answer one question: what is this building supposed to produce?

Not "what can we shoot here." That's a capabilities question. The production question is: what type of work, at what volume, for what kind of clients, needs to flow through this building every week for it to be financially healthy?

At Shear, the answer was: eCommerce content, Amazon Live production, livestream commerce, podcast and influencer content, and corporate multi-stream events. Those are five different production environments with five different technical requirements, five different crew workflows, and five different client-relationship models.

Every design decision at Shear was filtered through that answer.

## Soundstages that work as revenue units

The temptation with soundstages is to make them as big and flexible as possible. "Flexible" is the wrong word for a business. Flexible means the room can't do anything particularly well.

The stages at Shear are sized and configured for the specific production types that generate the most throughput. eCommerce content shoots differently than a live-shopping segment, which shoots differently than a podcast, which shoots differently than a corporate multi-stream event. The stage dimensions, lighting grid heights, power distribution, and acoustic treatment are all matched to the actual work, not to a hypothetical client who might need something someday.

## The live-shopping environment

This one required the most thought because it's the newest format and there's no established template to copy.

Live shopping — Amazon Live, TikTok Shop, and the platforms coming behind them — requires a hybrid environment: broadcast production standards in a space that's intimate enough to feel like a real conversation, not a QVC set. The lighting has to support both the product and the host. The camera positions have to allow for spontaneous interaction while keeping every shot clean. The room has to be reconfigurable fast because the format rewards volume.

We designed the live-shopping environment at Shear for speed. Changeover from one brand to the next in under an hour. That's not an accident. It's a design decision with a revenue number attached to it.

## The control room as the hub

A building that hosts multiple production environments simultaneously needs a central nervous system.

The control room at Shear manages multi-stream event production for the building. That means multiple cameras, multiple feeds, multiple destinations, from one room. It also means the team can support a live-shopping segment in one suite, a podcast recording in another, and a corporate live event on the stage, at the same time, without the operations of each bleeding into the others.

This is the part of studio design that most people skip because it's expensive and invisible to the client during a walkthrough. It's also the thing that makes the building actually work at the volume needed to justify the investment.

## What makes a studio a system vs. a room

The difference is whether the building was designed around the workflow or the workflow was figured out after the building was done.

A studio that was designed around the workflow has:
- Predictable changeover times between productions
- Crew routing that doesn't cross client paths
- Power and data infrastructure positioned for the actual equipment, not for a general-purpose "production environment"
- Acoustic separation between spaces that actually has to produce simultaneous content
- A booking and operations system that matches the physical constraints of the building

A studio that figured it out afterward has a beautiful floor plan and a scheduling problem that never goes away.

## Why I consult on studio builds now

The work at Shear, and before it at 211 Studio, is the kind of thing that's hard to learn anywhere else because most people building studios have either done it once or are working from a template that doesn't match what they're actually building.

I've done it twice, in two completely different production environments, and made different decisions both times based on what the building was supposed to produce.

If you're designing a studio and the design isn't starting from the question "what does this building need to produce to be financially healthy," start over. The room will be fine. The operating system won't.
