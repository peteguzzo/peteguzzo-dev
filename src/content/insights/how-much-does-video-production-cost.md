"How much does a video cost?" is the question that either starts a real conversation or ends one, depending on whether both sides understand what's actually being asked.

The honest answer is: it depends. But not in a way that's meant to be evasive. It depends on specific things that can be defined. Here's how to think about it.

## Start With the Production Day

The easiest way to build a video budget is to break it into production days. Everything scales from there.

There are typically two cost structures: studio production and location production. Studio tends to be more predictable. You control the environment, the equipment is already there, and you're not paying for location scouting, permits, or the uncertainty of weather and access. Location production adds variables, and those variables cost money to manage.

Neither is inherently more expensive than the other until you define the specifics. A location shoot at a client's own facility can be very affordable. A studio day at a high-end facility with a full equipment package is not.

## The Pre-Production Investment

Pre-production is where money spent saves money on set. Script development, location scouting, casting, shot lists, call sheets, logistics planning: every hour invested here reduces the chance of expensive problems during production.

The projects that blow budgets are almost always projects where pre-production was underfunded or rushed. What looks like a production-day cost is usually a pre-production gap.

## Post-Production: Where It Holds Together or Falls Apart

Post-production, editing, color grade, sound mix, graphics, revisions, is where the work becomes a deliverable. It's also where scope creep is most dangerous.

The cost of post is kept in check by two things: a well-executed production that delivers clean material to edit, and a clearly defined revision process. Unlimited rounds of revisions are not a feature. They're a cost center.

## What You're Actually Buying

The budget conversation should be a scope conversation. What are you trying to accomplish? Who's the audience? Where is this running? What does success look like? Those answers determine the format, the scale, and the production approach, and from there, the budget follows logically.

"How much does a video cost?" starts at a few hundred dollars and runs into the millions. The question that matters is: what does *your* video need to be?
